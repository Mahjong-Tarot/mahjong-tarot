## Purple Star

Bill is going deep on Purple Star to finish the parts that were never fully built out. The luck rating was relatively easy to do earlier, but the fortune (fate) part of the reading is far more complicated, and AI is now making it possible to tackle. He can see why he never finished it years ago.

His method is to go back to basics: dissect every Chinese character in each star's name and ask for both literal and figurative meanings, rather than trusting existing books and translations. He wants a true handle on the original intent. He is also researching the origins, noting that Purple Star readings were originally reserved for royalty, with the stars representing the emperor, the empress, and the figures around the emperor and their traits and functions. He wants to understand how the system actually started, not just what various astrologers claim, since many star names do not make sense to him and astrologers tend to differentiate themselves with their own naming.

On the production side, Bill is working on organizing 4 to 6 fields or topics to cover for each palace. Content-wise, the Purple Star articles go live this week, building on last week's discussion.

## Tao Te Ching

Bill is roughly one third of the way through a fresh retranslation of the Tao Te Ching. He has considered himself a Taoist for about 30 years, so the text resonates with him, and as an English teacher he finds most existing translations unclear, overly poetic, and failing to communicate the actual ideas. His goal is a translation a common person anywhere in the world can understand, not one requiring a master's or PhD in Chinese studies. When using AI to edit, he asks for phrasing that is "Google translatable" so a global audience can follow it.

The project is structured in two parts. The left-hand page carries the translation; the right-hand page gives three to five actionable things anyone can apply almost daily using Taoist wisdom. The book is meant to be digested and applied, not just read as theory.

He argues the work matters because Lao Tzu wrote in down-to-earth examples (a teapot that must be emptied before it can be refilled, wood carvings, spokes on a wheel), aimed at common people, but most translations bury that literal meaning under abstract, figurative language.

The central concept he wants to surface is Wu Wei, often misunderstood as "go with the flow" or taking the easy path. Bill defines it as "doing by not doing," or effortless action, and ties it to the modern idea of flow. His illustration: a champion surfer on the North Shore looks like he is barely doing anything while riding the curl, which is Wu Wei, versus the flailing surfer who falls in. Most people stumble through life flailing rather than acting in harmony with the natural and human world. He says even this single idea can change a person's life.
