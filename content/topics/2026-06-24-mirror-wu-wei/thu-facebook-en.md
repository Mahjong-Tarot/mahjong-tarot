The point of the Tao Te Ching was never to be admired.

It was to be used.

Lao Tzu wrote for common people making ordinary decisions. He gave them plain images because plain images are what sticks. The teapot is not a metaphor you need to decode. You've emptied one. You know exactly what that moment feels like and why it's necessary.

That's what bothered me about existing translations for years: they turn something immediately useful into something you need to sit with for a graduate seminar. That's a waste of a genuinely good idea.

So the book I'm writing has a specific structure. The translation is on the left-hand page. The right-hand page gives three to five things you can actually do with it, applicable on almost any given day and not just on a meditation retreat.

Wisdom you can use on a Tuesday beats wisdom you can only quote at a dinner party.

That's the standard I'm holding the Tao Te Ching to. And it's the same standard the Mahjong Mirror was built on.
