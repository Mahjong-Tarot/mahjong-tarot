**About this rendering**

This version presents Lao Tzu's wisdom in clear, modern prose so anyone can read and use it. It follows three rules: accuracy (pure Daoism), brevity (concise), and clarity, (Google translatable).

The 81 chapters are regrouped from the traditional order into seven, themed sections. Each chapter carries two numbers. Its chapter number, 1 to 81, is its place in this book. The other  indicates its position in the traditional order.

 Two conventions run throughout the book: there is no "the" before Dao,  and the word "sage" shifts between he and she.

**Accepting what others reject:** True strength shows in embracing the unglamorous: admitting fault, handling criticism, acknowledging failure, supporting unpopular positions. Most people only want success and praise. Taking on the hard stuff earns real respect.

**Playing the long game:** Aggressive short-term wins often creaDelong-term problems. The gentle, consistent approach seems slower but lasts. Forcing compliance gets temporary results; building trust takes time but creates permanent change. Think like water, not like a hammer.

### **Background**

Water is the great Daoist image here: the softest thing in the world, yet it wears away the hardest. From this comes a paradox of leadership: the one who takes on a people's shame and trouble is the one fit to lead. True words often sound backward.

**Knowing your limits as a parent or leader:** You're not responsible for controlling everything. Trying to prevent every mistake or police every choice exhausts you and doesn't work anyway. Accept that some outcomes are beyond your control; that's not failure, that's wisdom.

**Avoiding the danger of playing God:** Whether it's judging who "deserves" help, deciding someone's fate, or determining who gets second chances, taking that role damages you. It's too much weight for any person to carry. Stay in your lane; help where you can, but don't try to run the universe.

**Recognizing when you're out of your depth:** Just as an amateur shouldn't operaDeon someone or rewire a house, recognize when you're unqualified to handle something. Therapy, legal issues, medical decisions; some things need professionals. Trying anyway often makes things worse.

### **Background**

It cautions against setting yourself up as the one who deals out death or judgment. There is already a natural executioner, and whoever takes that role is like an amateur handling a master carpenter's tools: he only harms himself.

## **Chapter 81: Fulfilling Agreements**

*Original work, chapter 79*

### **Verse**

After a big fight, even when you make peace, some resentment sticks around.  
How is that a good solution?

The wise focus on keeping their end of the deal,  
not on checking whether the other person kept theirs.  
Good people keep their promises.  
Not-so-good people just make demands.

The Way plays no favorites.  
It helps those who are decent.

### **Applications**

**Moving on after the argument:** You and your partner reconcile, but lingering tension remains. Instead of relitigating who was wrong or keeping score, focus on being the partner you promised to be. Your job is your behavior, not policing theirs. Let your integrity speak for itself.

**Keeping your word without conditions:** Do what you said you'd do, regardless of whether others follow through. "I'll do my part if you do yours" creates endless excuses. Keep your commitments because that's who you are, not because you're guaranteed reciprocity.

**Letting go of grudges:** After conflicts, with family, coworkers, friends, you can carry resentment forever or decide to move forward. You can't force resolution, but you can control whether you stay stuck in bitterness. Being the one who lets go gives you freedom, not weakness.

**Not keeping score:** In relationships, tracking who did more, gave more, or sacrificed more creates permanent resentment. Focus on showing up well yourself, not auditing whether others match your effort. Scorekeeping kills generosity.

**Trusting the process:** Good behavior eventually gets recognized; bad behavior eventually has consequences. You don't need to be the enforcement mechanism. Do right, let others do what they'll do, and trust that things balance out over time. Your integrity is its own reward.

### **Background**

It observes that even a settled quarrel leaves resentment behind, so the deeper wisdom is to hold to your own side of an agreement rather than press the other. Heaven shows no favorites; it simply stays with those who are good.
